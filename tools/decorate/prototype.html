Decorating the map, after the paint.

Painting rewrote the surface material. Decoration adds the surface life — flora scattered on the soil, worn paths dragged across it, boulders and trees seated on top, ponds and channels carved in. Five prototypes, every figure generated by the real algorithm the stage would run.

Orientation

One more pass over the realized world

A generated layout compiles into a SketchLayout (the geometry) and a MapIntent (the objectives). The rasterizer turns the geometry into a stone VoxelWorld; the structure stampers seat rooms, cubes and objectives on it; then terrain paint (G157) rewrites the stone surface into grass, dirt, quartz, clay. Every one of those is a pass in realize. Decoration is the next pass in the same chain — it reads the finished, painted world and dresses it.

Anvil .mca

Terrain paint's one rule is “no new geometry, only materials”. Decoration is the opposite half of G34: it does add geometry — a block of tall grass in the air cell above the soil, a tree, a rock. So it is a sibling pass, not a new terrain material. (Note: in this codebase “decorative” already names non-playable floating masses and non-objective wool — the code family here is dressing to keep those apart.)

What every pass shares

The four prototypes below look different but run on the same four primitives — which is the argument for building them as one stage rather than four features:

NOISE

The same deterministic PatternNoise paint already uses — a density field, a clumping field, a per-cell dice roll, keyed by seed + cell.

MASK

Eligibility from the painted surface: flora only over soil, props off the objectives — read from the top block and the plan's protected regions.

SCATTER

Blue-noise sampling — evenly spaced sites for rocks and trees — from local maxima of a hash field, no overlaps, stable across re-exports.

STAMP

A 3-D volume seated on SurfaceTop and written with SetBlock — the same shape-mask-in-a-box the objective stampers already use.

1Flora overlay

A paint-aware overlay command

The plainest decoration is the one every WorldEdit user knows: //overlay — “put 70% air, 30% grass-and-fern on top of everything.” The studio can do better than everything, because it just painted the surface and knows what each cell is. Grass and fern belong over grass-block, dirt, coarse dirt, podzol and (sparsely) sand. Over the quartz of a plaza or the wool of a monument, nothing. The overlay is masked by the paint underneath it.

Two knobs do the work: a density field decides which eligible cells get anything at all, and a species mix decides what. Both are noise — the same Fbm/Value/Unit the terrain patterns are built from — so the choice between a sparse speckle and a lush meadow is one field and one threshold.

The field, drawn

Everything starts here. This is a fractal-noise density field — bright is dense, dark is bare. A flat threshold on it is what separates “meadow” from “clearing”. Raise the octaves for detail; raise the scale for larger patches.

Three ways to scatter

The kind of noise decides the read. White noise is an even TV-static speckle. Value noise clumps into meadows and clearings. Blue noise spaces plants evenly with no two touching — the look of deliberately placed decoration. All three are the same overlay; only the field changes.

Masked by the paint, and the species mix

Left: the overlay run over a mixed painted surface — a grass field, a sand wash, a quartz plaza, a dirt scar. Flora lands only where the ground accepts it; the plaza and the paths stay clean. Right: within the vegetated cells, a second low-frequency field paints flower fields — poppies and dandelions cluster where it peaks, so the map gets meadows of colour instead of confetti.

Reuses / net-new

  • reuse  PatternNoise.Fbm/Value/Unit for the density, flower and dice fields — the exact machinery NoiseMaterial already thresholds.
  • reuse  the SurfaceTop XZ grid for elevation and the top block for eligibility (soil vs. quartz), read like TerrainProfile reads columns.

2Paths

Drag a line; the ground remembers it

The sketch lasso already captures a freeform drag as a polyline — it just closes it into a filled ring. A path is the open version: keep the centerline, and buffer it. Every cell within a radius of the nearest segment becomes path material, replacing the surface finish beneath it. That radius test — dist ≤ R to the nearest segment, a swept-disc union — is the “Voronoi radius” drawn directly; the disc-scan primitive already exists in Skeleton.

A single band is the boring case. The interesting paths are the imperfect ones — worn to gravel in patches, edged with rough cobble, or broken into stepping stones across a gap. Each is the same distance field with one extra gate.

Reuses / net-new

  • reuse  the lasso capture in sketch-draw-controller.js (#startLasso/#addLassoPoint) — the drag is identical; only the release differs.
  • reuse  SketchShape.Vertices for the centerline and its existing Radius field for the half-width; the disc-scan dx²+dz² ≤ r² from Skeleton.Anchored.
  • new  a "path" branch in SketchRasterizer.RingOf/RasterShape (band ring, or capsule fill) + its toRing twin in geometry/shape.js, kept in lock-step by a parity constant.
  • new  the gates — a worn dice, an FBM edge (kin to the G142 roughen operators), and an along-arc stepping-stone sampler.
3Boulders

Rocks, half-buried and scattered

A boulder is the first decoration that is genuinely 3-D — a volume, not an overlay. But it is the same shape-mask-in-a-box the objective stampers already build: seat a BlockBox on SurfaceTop, then fill the cells that pass an ellipsoid test. Half of the ellipsoid sits below the surface, so the rock reads as emerging from the ground rather than dropped on it. Perturb the radius with noise for an angular, weathered read; stack two or three for a cairn.

Shape families

Side elevation — the ground line is where the surface sits; everything below it is buried. Four masks over the same box.

Finishes

The same rounded mask, four materials — and moss creeping onto the top-lit faces, itself a tiny noise mask (the finish carries its own micro-flora).

Scattered, with exclusions

Top-down. Blue-noise sites give even spacing; a size field makes some big, some small. The protected regions of the plan — spawns, objectives, the paths from §2 — are exclusion masks: no rock lands on them. This is the same scatter trees will use.

4Trees

Two ways to grow a tree

The trivial tree copies vanilla: a trunk of a known height and a canopy of a known shape, parameterised per species. That gets you an oak grove for free and is genuinely all most maps need. The interesting tree is grown — a recursive branch skeleton with a handful of knobs, so the studio can design a shape no vanilla generator makes. Both are the same stamper as the boulder: a trunk column and a leaf mask over a box.

Vanilla templates

Side elevation. Each is a parametric template — trunk height, canopy radius, canopy profile — not a copied schematic.

The grown tree

Not a fractal, and not a straight pole. The skeleton is recursive — ez-tree's levels, children and per-level taper, with an upward "force" — but every branch is a gnarly stepped path smoothed into a flowing Catmull-Rom curve, so the trunk wanders like a bonsai (up, twist, straighten) and limbs branch to a second degree. The spine on the left is the view the tree is designed against — the centerlines and their thickness, each limb's spline in its own colour — before any blocks go on. The stems knob gives the trunk setups: one stem that forks, a double, or a triple base. The leader knob decides whether the trunk dies at the fork (low — a spreading, decurrent form) or climbs on as a single central axis through the crown, thinning and twisting with branches staggered up it (high — a birch/conifer read); the trunk no longer just stops as if sawn. Height (shown in blocks) scales the whole tree — and with it the stem thickness at the foot and the branch count, since a short branch is terminal, so a sapling reads as a few clusters on a thin stalk rather than a shrunken big tree.

The crown is placed the way a mapmaker does it by hand — because the naive sphere-with-holes is the thing that looks generated. Each branch tip gets one dense disc-shaped cluster (a flat leaf-rock, ~90% full), pushed up and out from the tip, never down toward the trunk, since leaves sit at the ends and not on the wood. Neighbouring clusters keep a seam of air between them — a cell fills only when it clearly belongs to one cluster — so every patch reads as its own branch's instead of one merged blob, which is the whole reason to keep the branch count low. A few strands hang below for a broken lower edge.

Parameter sweep

A height gradient — a knee-high sapling up to a full tree — then a double and triple base. Height is not just a scale: a smaller tree has a thinner stem at the foot and drops branches (a short limb is terminal), so a sapling is a few clusters on a thin stalk, not a shrunken big tree.

A forest, not a field

Top-down. A low-frequency density field gathers trees into groves with clearings between — a forest reads as clumps, never an even orchard. Same blue-noise scatter as the boulders, gated by the density field, exclusions honoured.

Reuses / net-new

  • reuse  the boulder's DecorationStamper — a tree is trunk column + leaf mask; the same BlockBox, SurfaceYOver seating and blue-noise scatter.
  • reuse  the §2 path's swept-disc as the limb primitive — a Catmull-Rom spline capsule is that fill lifted to 3-D, so the tree and the path share one rasterizer.
  • reuse  the §3 boulder's dense disc as the leaf cluster — the crown is one carved disc per tip with air seams between clusters (nearest-cluster ownership), not a spherical brush.
  • reuse  the density-field-gated scatter from §3 for grove clumping — one low-frequency Fbm.
  • new  the species templates (data rows) and the recursive spline grower — a flowing Catmull-Rom skeleton with stems · trunk flow · branch angle · levels · leaf size — as a DecorationStyle variant, designed against the spine view.
5Ponds & channels

Water is a path that carves — and levels

A channel starts where the §2 path does: a dragged centerline and a radius, the same swept-disc band. But water cannot drape over the surface the way gravel can — it has to sit in a carved bed and fill to a level plane, or it reads as blue paint on a hill. So a channel digs a trench, banks the sides, and fills to one water height; a pond is the closed version — an organic basin, the §3 boulder blob turned concave. The rest is the read: depth (shallow at the edge, dark in the middle), a shoreline band, and edge life — reeds on the bank, lily pads on still water.

Why it isn't just a blue path

The same stroke, two tools, in cross-section. The path lays its material on the existing surface and moves on. The channel cuts a bed below the surface and fills it to a level line — the one thing a draped path can never do, and the reason water is its own tool.

Channels

Drag the centerline, get a watercourse. A canal holds clean banks; a natural edge wanders with the same FBM as the §2 rough path; a stream narrows and shallows into riffles. The shore is its own irregular pass — its width wanders, and drops to zero in places so the water meets the land with no border; the bank and bed are a pattern (sand · pale gravel · coarse dirt), and the bottom shows through the shallows. Depth is a smooth field warped off-centre, so one side runs deeper. Reeds line the bank; lily pads settle on the still, deep water.

Ponds

A pond is a closed organic basin — the boulder blob with an FBM-wandering outline, read concave. Size sets the water body; shore is the same irregular, sometimes-zero band as the channel, with a patterned bank; edge sets how ragged the outline is; and edge-life scatters reeds around the bank and lily pads across the open water. Depth runs off-centre, not a clean bowl.

Put together

Two ponds joined by a channel — the whole system: basins, a watercourse between them, one level surface, banks and edge life throughout.

Reuses / net-new

  • reuse  the §2 path's centerline + swept-disc for a channel; the §3 boulder blob + FBM edge for a pond's organic outline; the §1 flora overlay for reeds on the bank.
  • reuse  the blue-noise scatter for lily pads and for placing ponds in low ground, with the plan's protected regions as exclusions.

Summary

What already exists, and what the stage adds

The prototypes lean hard on machinery the terrain-paint work (G157) and the sketch tools already shipped. The net-new surface is small and well-shaped — one stamper, one rasterizer branch, one overlay pass, all in the same realize seam.

Carve it off G34, the way G157 was. Terrain paint began as “the terrain slice” of G34 and earned its own doc (terrain-painting.md) and rule prefix (TP*). This is the sibling “prop stamps” slice: its own doc under docs/world-export/, its own DR* family, and a pass wired last into SketchWorldBuilder.Build, after the painter.

Prototype figures generated in-page by the same algorithm shapes the stage would run — deterministic noise (mirroring PatternNoise), true distance-to-segment paths, blue-noise scatter, Catmull-Rom limb splines swept with the path's own disc. Nothing here is hand-drawn. The C# implementations are the authority; this is the sketch.
